Search playbook

Searching well is a skill. Here it is.

Most people search for funding the way they search for a restaurant, and then conclude there is nothing out there. There is a great deal out there. It is indexed badly, split across a dozen systems, and described in the funder’s vocabulary rather than yours.

1. Where funding actually lives

No single system contains everything. Knowing which system holds which kind of money is most of the skill.

2. Building a keyword string that works

Grants.gov keyword search is literal. It does not know that your “community-engaged research” is the funder’s “stakeholder partnership,” and it will happily return zero results while $40M sits one synonym away.

The method

  1. Write your project in three vocabularies. Yours, the funder’s, and the policy world’s. “Neighborhood disinvestment” (yours), “community development” (funder), “place-based revitalization” (policy). Search all three.
  2. Search the narrowest term first. If it returns nothing, broaden one step at a time. Starting broad and filtering down buries you.
  3. Use quoted phrases and OR. "program evaluation" OR "evaluation research" OR "impact evaluation" works far better than evaluation, which returns everything.
  4. Search by method, not just topic. Funders often name the method: “randomized controlled trial,” “quasi-experimental,” “mixed methods,” “community-based participatory research,” “implementation science.”
  5. Search by population. “rural,” “under-resourced,” “historically underserved,” “first-generation,” “limited-resource farmers,” “justice-involved.”
  6. Search by ALN (CFDA) number. Once you find one relevant program, search its ALN to surface everything else under that authority. This is the highest-yield single trick on this page.
  7. Search by agency, empty keyword. Browse everything an agency has open. Ten minutes, and you will find things no keyword would have surfaced.
  8. Include forecasted opportunities. A forecast is a gift — months of lead time to build the team before anyone else starts writing.
The reverse search. Find three funded projects like yours in RePORTER or NSF Award Search. Note the program, the mechanism, the ALN, and the program officer. Then go find that program’s current announcement. You are now searching from a known-good example rather than guessing at vocabulary.

3. Reading a NOFO in the right order

Do not read a funding announcement front to back. Read it in the order that lets you quit fastest.

  1. First — 2 minutesEligibility

    Institution type, PI requirements, citizenship, career stage, prior-award limits, cost share. If you are not eligible, you are done. Most people read this fourth and lose an afternoon.

  2. Second — 3 minutesAward information

    Number of awards, size, period, and total program funding. Divide total by award size — that is roughly how many will be funded. If it is three awards nationally, know that now.

  3. Third — 10 minutesThe review criteria

    Section 6 in the current NOFO structure. This is what you are actually being graded on. Note the weights. Your page allocation should roughly follow the points.

  4. Fourth — 15 minutesProgram description

    Now, knowing what you are graded on, read what they want. Look for the priorities stated in passing — they are usually the real ones.

  5. FifthApplication contents and format

    Build the compliance matrix here. Every page limit, every attachment, every certification.

  6. SixthEverything referenced but not included

    General administrative requirements, addenda, program-wide terms, agency-wide policy documents. These carry their own kill conditions and people routinely skip them.

  7. Then — before writingDecide

    Run the Go / No-Go screen. A medium fit is usually a no.

4. Setting up alerts so it comes to you

Grants.gov saved searches

Create a free Grants.gov account, build a search with your best keyword string and eligibility filters, and save it. You get email when anything new matches. Build three or four narrow ones rather than one broad one — a broad alert becomes noise you stop reading, which is worse than no alert.

NIH Guide weekly

Subscribe to the NIH Guide weekly table of contents. It is the only reliable way to see NIH notices, and policy notices (like changes to review or AI policy) appear there first. Skim the notices section even when nothing applies to you.

Agency listservs and RSS

NSF publishes updates by directorate. IES, NIJ, NEH, and IMLS all run announcement lists. Sign up for the two or three closest to your work. Dear Colleague Letters from NSF often have short fuses and appear nowhere else.

Foundation watching

Foundations do not syndicate. Pick six that fit your work and check them quarterly, or subscribe to their newsletters. Note their cycle — many post the same call the same month every year, so you can plan a year out.

This site

Star programs in the curated directory to build a shortlist, then export the deadlines as an .ics file with 30-day reminders. Save a filtered search as a link and email it to a colleague.

Your research office

Most research offices circulate opportunity digests and hold a subscription to Pivot-RP or a similar database that individual faculty cannot afford. Ask. At VSU, Research Development is Dr. Lora Battle Bailey.

5. Limited submissions and internal competitions

Some programs cap how many applications a single institution may submit — sometimes one. If you discover this two weeks out, you have already lost, because the internal selection deadline was months earlier.

How to protect yourself: the moment a program interests you, search the announcement for “limited submission,” “per institution,” “only one application,” “nominat,” and “institutional.” If any appear, email your research office that day. Ask two questions: is there an internal competition, and what is its deadline? Then ask a third: is there a list of limited-submission opportunities you can be added to?

At VSU specifically: no limited-submission policy, internal competition process, or opportunity list appears anywhere on the public site. That does not mean nothing is happening — it means you must ask OSRP and Research Development directly rather than assume. See the list of things to confirm.

Common limited-submission programs: NSF S-STEM and major center awards, NIH institutional training grants (T32) and instrumentation (S10), NEH Summer Stipends (usually two nominations per campus), many foundation programs including Sloan, Beckman, Searle, and Research Corporation, and most institutional infrastructure awards.

The honest read on the category: discovery tools have clear value. The AI drafting tools are the least mature and the most legally exposed segment. The genuinely underexploited category is analytics used for targeting — which is free.

7. First-time applicant checklist

  1. Meet your research office before you have a project. They are not a rubber stamp; they know which programs your institution wins and which internal deadlines will surprise you.
  2. Get your registrations done now. eRA Commons, Research.gov, an ORCID. They take days you will not have later.
  3. File your conflict-of-interest disclosure and finish RCR training. At many institutions, including VSU, an outstanding disclosure will hold your submission.
  4. Read fifteen funded abstracts in your target program on RePORTER or NSF Award Search. Notice the scale, the framing, the team composition. This calibrates you faster than any workshop.
  5. Start smaller than you think. A seed grant, a foundation small grant, a state award, or a subaward on someone else’s project builds the track record that reviewers look for. Reviewers ask whether you have done this before.
  6. Email a program officer. Under 200 words, two or three answerable questions, a one-page concept attached. Use the PO contact prompt.
  7. Find a mentor who has held the award you want. Ask to read their funded application and their summary statement. The summary statement teaches more than the application.
  8. Write the one-page aims first and workshop it ten weeks out, before the rest exists.
  9. Schedule a Red Team. Put it on the calendar the day you decide to apply, not the week you need it.
  10. Plan to be rejected. Most first applications are not funded. The summary statement is the most valuable document you will get all year — a resubmission written from it starts with an enormous structural advantage.
One last thing. The most common reason people do not get funded is that they do not apply. The second is that they apply to the wrong thing. Both are fixable in an afternoon with a good search and an honest Go / No-Go.
