Foundation 990s

Every foundation says it funds your field. The tax return says what it wrote cheques for.

A private foundation must file Form 990-PF every year, and it is public. It reports what the foundation actually paid out, what it is sitting on, and — in the PDF — the name of every organisation that received money. Fifteen minutes here can save you six weeks on a letter of inquiry to a funder that was never going to say yes.

Read it in this order. First, scale: if a foundation pays out $400,000 a year across all causes, your $250,000 ask is not realistic. Second, the operating flag: an operating foundation spends its money running its own programmes and usually accepts no outside proposals at all. Third, the payout rate: private non-operating foundations must distribute roughly 5% of investment assets annually, so one sitting well below that is either newly formed or not in a giving posture. Only then open the PDF and read the grantee list — that is where you learn whether they fund institutions like yours.

What this data can and cannot tell you. The figures below come from the IRS extract of each filed return, republished by ProPublica. Two limits worth knowing. Returns lag — the most recent year available is usually one to two years behind, so a foundation that changed direction last year will not show it yet. And the machine-readable extract carries totals only; the list of who actually received grants exists only inside the PDF of the return, which is why every year below links to its PDF. For a private foundation the grantee list is Part XV. It is worth the scroll.

Community foundations are a different animal. Most file the ordinary Form 990 rather than a 990-PF, and the IRS extract for Form 990 does not include a grants-paid figure at all. When you look one up here you will see revenue, expenses and assets but no giving total — that is a gap in the federal data, not evidence that the organisation gives nothing. For those, total expenses is the usable proxy, and the PDF still holds the detail.
