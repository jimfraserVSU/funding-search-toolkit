Simulated review panels

Get reviewed before you get reviewed.

Copy a prompt into Claude or any capable AI assistant, paste your draft, and get a panel that scores you on the actual criteria with the actual scale — NIH’s three factors and 1–9, NSF’s five elements across two criteria, IES’s two opposite-facing scales, a 100-point NOFO rating-factor tally. Then fill in the rating card and take it to a real colleague.

Read this before you use any of it. These prompts are for your own draft, before you submit it. That is pre-submission institutional work, and no agency rule reaches it.

Two hard lines you must not cross:

1. Never put someone else’s application into an AI tool. If you serve on a study section or an NSF panel, NIH and NSF both flatly prohibit uploading any part of an application, review, or critique to a generative AI tool — NIH treats it as a violation of the confidentiality agreement you signed. Keep that workflow completely separate from this one.

2. AI can critique your writing. It cannot be the author of your science. As of receipt dates on or after September 25, 2025, NIH will not consider applications “substantially developed by AI” or containing “sections substantially developed by AI.” NSF folded AI-mediated fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism into its research misconduct definition effective December 8, 2025, and encourages you to disclose AI use in the Project Description. Unverified AI-generated citations can constitute fabrication at both agencies. Use these tools as a reviewer and an editor — verify every fact and every citation yourself.

Does mock review actually work? The evidence is encouraging and imperfect. Johns Hopkins Neurology’s internal grant review program reported 58% funding for internally reviewed applications versus 22% for unreviewed ones in the same department. Columbia’s School of Nursing reported 42% versus 20%, on a $127,000 five-year investment that preceded $3 million in external funding. A mock-review program for early-career investigators reported 37% success across 212 participants.

Every one of those comparisons is self-selected — faculty who volunteer for a nine-month bootcamp differ from those who don’t. The honest claim is that internal review is associated with substantially higher success and shows strong return on investment, not that it causes a 2.5× improvement. Run it anyway. The downside is a few hours.

How to sequence them

The mistake almost every academic mock review makes is holding one big event two weeks out, which produces feedback nobody has time to act on. Government contracting solved this decades ago: gate each review to a draft maturity level, and give each one a different question.

Two details worth stealing. Michigan’s R01 Boot Camp pays external reviewers a $500 honorarium — it is the difference between a perfunctory read and a real one — and runs a session called “Everything But The Science” covering budget, biosketch, facilities, letters, and compliance as a first-class deliverable rather than an afterthought. IES assigns a synthesizer who reads back a summary of the discussion before final scoring, which forces the panel to converge on the actual score drivers rather than a list of grievances.

The prompt library

Build a rating card

Run the simulated panel, then fill this in — or hand it to a colleague and have them fill it in cold. Pick the funder’s real scale; the card adapts. It stays in your browser and exports as a file you can circulate.

Running a real panel with real people

The 90-minute mock study section

Three reviewers, one chair, one synthesizer. Send the draft and the criteria two weeks ahead. On the day: each reviewer states preliminary scores before any discussion (this prevents anchoring), primary presents for 8 minutes, others add for 4 each, 20 minutes open discussion, the synthesizer reads back the score drivers, everyone re-scores privately. The applicant listens and does not defend — that rule matters more than any other.

The 30-minute aims review

Cheapest high-value thing you can do. Ten-minute presentation of the aims page only, ten minutes of questions, written feedback within a week. Run it ten to twelve weeks out, when the science can still change. Columbia built a program around exactly this and reported doubling their funding rate.

Recruiting reviewers

The best reviewer is someone who has served on the actual panel or held the actual award. Second best is someone from an adjacent field who will not grant you any assumed knowledge. Pay them if you can — even a modest honorarium changes the quality of the read. Ask for a specific deliverable and a specific date, not “thoughts.”

Learn review from the inside

Serving as a reviewer teaches more about grant writing than any workshop. NIH runs an Early Career Reviewer program, NSF recruits panelists continuously through program officers, and the W.T. Grant Foundation runs an Early-Career Reviewer Program. Email a program officer and say you are available.
